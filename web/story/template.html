1 · How satellites see fire

Two eyes in the sky, with different eyesight

A “hotspot” is a spot on the ground that the satellite sees as much hotter than its surroundings, usually a fire. MODIS has watched since 2000. VIIRS joined in 2012 with a sharper view.

1 km

MODIS sees in large squares

A small farm burn can get lost inside a 1 km square.

375 m

VIIRS sees in small squares

The same small fire fills more of its square, so it gets detected.

×4.8

So VIIRS counts more fires

Over Peru, VIIRS finds almost five times as many hotspots as MODIS. Comparing them directly would be unfair.

2 · One country, three worlds

Coast, Andes and Amazon burn at different times

Peru's climates are so different that one conversion for the whole country is not enough. Each region gets its own adjustment, with its own fire season.

3 · Making one record

Slide to harmonize

If we simply add up all satellites, fire seems to explode in 2012 and again in 2018. Those jumps are new satellites arriving, not more fire. Move the slider to convert everything to one VIIRS scale.

For scientists: the model
log(VIIRS + 1) = a + d · high_season + b · log(MODIS + 1)
  • Fit per region on monthly counts, 2012–2022 (both sensors active). Regions with fewer than 24 fire months use the national fit.
  • high_season = months whose average count is above the region's own monthly average.
  • Back-transform with Duan smearing; p10–p90 of residuals gives the range shown for pre-2012 estimates.
  • From 2012: one VIIRS satellite per day (NOAA-20 from April 2018, S-NPP before). Vegetation fires, nominal and high confidence.
